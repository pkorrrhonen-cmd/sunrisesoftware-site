Atlas · decision rooms

Before I settle how my software is built, I ask a panel of AI models for a second opinion.

When I have to decide how a feature or a part of my software is built, and the choice would be hard to undo, I ask my judging panel. Two AI agents from two different model families get the facts and the options, but never my choice. Each seals its first vote before it reads the other's, they argue in rounds, and two judging models score the arguments. If the panel disagrees with me, the decision comes back with its reasons, and I decide again. In Atlas, the shared record of everything I build, a panel is called a room.

In use · 2026 →

How a room is built

A room is a multi-agent debate with rules against the way such debates go wrong. Language models in a panel tend to agree with each other instead of checking each other, which the field calls sycophancy and conformity, and published comparisons find that a debate often does no better than asking one model several times, with mixed model families doing better. So the two seats come from two families, Claude and GPT, and each seals its first vote before it can read the other's; the votes are revealed together. Then come two rounds of arguments, in which a seat can have a web page fetched and kept as evidence. Between the rounds two judging models score the arguments, not the votes: Jev, a fast judging model from TypeSafe, whose score counts, and Clef, on trial, whose readings are kept but not counted.

The room does not average its seats. It reads each option at the seat that supports it least and picks the option whose weakest support is highest. That is the maximin rule (Wald, 1950): one enthusiastic seat cannot carry an option the other barely accepts, and a low value is the room saying it is unsure.

The room in the video

On 17 Oct 2026 I decided which secret scanner the security gate of my coding workbench should use, and from which release. The room had the scanner's releases page fetched and found that the release I had chosen did not exist yet, only a release candidate. It chose the same scanner on a condition: pin the release candidate and its rule file together, and let the gate refuse anything it cannot read. Its value was only 0.15, far under the bar of 0.6 written down before the room ran, so I read it as a warning rather than as the room knowing better. I took the condition 41 minutes after the room closed, and the gate's first code, the next day, was pinned exactly as the room asked.

The challenger

Since 30 Oct 2026 a room can take a third agent that never votes. Each round this challenger writes up to three objections to the option in the lead, the judges read whether each one carries weight, and both agents have to answer every one that does before their final vote. It is the devil's advocate, close to dialectical inquiry, two devices that group-decision research found better than plain consensus (Schweiger, Sandberg and Ragan, 1986). The same research carries a warning I keep in view: an assigned devil's advocate can harden the view it attacks, and a genuine minority did better (Nemeth, Brown and Rogers, 2001). My challenger also comes from the same family as one of the agents and shares its blind spots, so whether it helps is measured over the rooms, not assumed.

What the record shows, and what it cannot

Every room leaves a record, and every decision it checks is counted. By 13 Nov 2026 the rooms had checked 335 of my decisions and disagreed with me on 90, about one in four. Who was right shows only when the world answers, so each outcome is recorded as it arrives. The first test I pre-registered has read its twenty outcomes: none of those decisions was overturned, so it could not tell a confident room from an unsure one. A stricter test, locked on 27 Oct 2026, counts only outcomes thirty days old, and on 13 Nov 2026 it had none. The judges need the same scrutiny: Jev found weight in 272 of 309 objections, the leniency known from work on LLM-as-a-judge, so that reading separates little. Until the outcomes come in, a room is a second opinion I can show, not one I can prove right.
