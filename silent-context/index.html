Since AI 2026 · Elisa's challenge

An agent that knows where its advice came from.

Silent context reads what field engineers and network experts write to each other, binds every piece of advice to the words it came from and to the part of the network it is about, and lets an agent use it the next time the same fault appears. I built it alone over three days with AI tools, for Elisa's challenge at Since AI 2026 in Turku.

Built 6 to 8 Nov 2026 · third of fifteen

What Elisa asked

Elisa runs a large part of Finland's telecom network, and more of it runs itself every year. What the field engineers and network experts learn about it stays in their conversations: a chat thread, an email, someone's head. The challenge was to capture that knowledge without giving those people any extra work, tie it to the right part of the network with its source and the time it holds, and let an agent use it the next time a similar case appears.

What Silent context does

It reads the Teams and email exports as they are; nobody writes anything down for it. Each piece of advice becomes a claim in the expert's own words and language, tied to the units it is about, with the wording of its scope kept as it was said and two clocks: when it holds in the world, and when it was learned. Evidence arrives as dated events, so a claim can be confirmed, disputed or replaced, and you can ask what was known at any moment.

When the same fault appears on another unit, the agent proposes the expert's step before anyone replaces hardware or reboots, shows where the advice came from, and says when it does not know. A policy decides who acts: by default the agent proposes and a person acts, a reboot or a hardware replacement never goes to the agent, and there is no write path to any device. When the agent does not know, it drafts the question for the person on call, who sends it.

What I measured, and what I did not

160 tests run without a model or a key, from the recorded answers. On a truth set of 13 sources it found all 13. On a held-out set of 16 sources that the rule author never read, it bound one confidently wrong in the recorded run, and zero or one in each of five repeats. The network and most of the data are synthetic, as the challenge allowed, so none of this shows how it reads real conversations or binds in a real network.

Why third, in my own reading

Elisa's reviewers judged usability first, then linking precision, time validity and overall readiness. The two entries ahead of mine each built something a person types into, running live with real models: one answers in a text box, the other lives in the chat thread the engineers already use. Mine was a page of scenes that tells the story but cannot take a question, and it ran on recorded answers so that anyone could run it without a key. I also drew the line too tightly: I built more of the network's automation, the agent that acts under a policy, and they built more of the tool the person on call holds. The jury judged the layer people touch every day. Next time I read the brief from several sides before I draw the line, and I build the live path first.
